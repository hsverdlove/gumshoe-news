## US News | sting=us
[ava] Here at home, federal investigators are looking into a wave of suspicious letters mailed to congressional offices and state election offices, three weeks before Election Day. The F.B.I. and the Postal Inspection Service say some of the letters contained an unknown substance, and they're working to find whoever sent them. Senators were warned by the Senate Sergeant at Arms to treat these letters as potentially hazardous.
[ava] According to NBC News, the secretary of state's offices in Louisiana and Oklahoma received envelopes with white powder on Tuesday, and postal investigators intercepted one headed for Ohio. In Louisiana, an employee who opened one was taken to the hospital as a precaution, and police say no harmful substance has been found so far. No injuries have been reported anywhere. Officials in all three states say election work hasn't been affected. This has happened before: in twenty twenty-four, election offices in at least twenty states got similar powder letters.
[ava] Also today, the Justice Department says Kansas businessman Brett Blackman has been sentenced to nineteen years in prison for one of the largest telemedicine fraud schemes it has ever prosecuted. Prosecutors say his online platform churned out fake doctors' orders for back braces and other equipment that seniors didn't need, leading to more than a billion dollars in bills to Medicare and other federal programs, and more than four hundred fifty million dollars actually paid out. A jury convicted him in April.

## International | sting=world
[ava] Around the world, a tragedy in Pakistan. The roof of a school in Peshawar collapsed during morning classes today, killing at least thirty-five people. The A.P. says thirty-four of them were children, and one was a teacher. More than fifty were hurt. Police arrested the school's owner, and the prime minister has ordered an investigation into how the building was allowed to stay open.
[ava] In the Iran war, the fighting has spread to Yemen and Saudi Arabia. Yemen's government says the prime minister and cabinet survived a Houthi missile attack on the presidential palace in Aden, though two guards were reportedly killed. The Saudi-led coalition says it shot down a missile aimed at the holy city of Medina. And sources tell Reuters a Houthi strike on Tuesday badly damaged an office building at Saudi Aramco's headquarters in Dhahran, though no one was hurt and oil operations kept running.
[ava] Separately, CNN reports the Pentagon has drawn up plans that could use its largest non-nuclear bomb, nicknamed the mother of all bombs, against Iranian energy and nuclear sites if President Trump orders combat to resume. That's based on unnamed officials, and the Pentagon hasn't confirmed it. Iran, for its part, says it will keep fighting until the U.S. backs down.

## Technology | sting=tech
[andrew] In tech, OpenAI has backed down in its legal fight with Apple. Apple accuses OpenAI of taking trade secrets through fourteen employees who left Apple to join OpenAI. The Verge, citing Bloomberg, says that after the judge signaled he was highly likely to side with Apple, OpenAI voluntarily agreed not to use Apple's proprietary information while the case goes on, and to hand over copies of those employees' drives. That's not an admission, and the lawsuit itself is still going.
[andrew] And Amazon's Bee, the A.I. wearable that listens to your conversations and turns them into notes and to-do lists, has two new devices. The Bee Frontier, which you wear on your wrist or clip on, is seventy-five dollars. The Bee Eclipse is a card-sized version that sticks magnetically to the back of your phone for a hundred dollars. TechCrunch says audio isn't stored and transcripts are encrypted. Of course, privacy advocates have long worried about devices that record everyone around you, not just the person wearing them.

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
[ava] In A.I., Meta just made a big move. Just after one o'clock, Mark Zuckerberg posted that Meta is releasing the weights for Muse Spark Open, meaning anyone can download the model and run it themselves. Meta's A.I. chief Alexandr Wang says it has about eight hundred sixteen billion parameters, a one million token memory, and it's built for coding and multi-step tasks. Zuckerberg says it runs well on Apple's new Mac Studios, after work with Apple. Wang calls it the most capable American open model yet. That's Meta's own claim, and independent tests haven't come in.
[ava] Meanwhile, the A.I. money question. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, told investors its revenue hit about sixteen point eight billion dollars last quarter, up forty-five percent, and that it was cash-flow positive. The Wall Street Journal and others reported it. Bulls say that proves A.I. labs can make money. But one analyst at D.A. Davidson said the growth was slower than the quarter before and "not what anyone wanted to see," which is part of why chip stocks are under pressure. And chipmaker T.S.M.C., which builds the chips for Nvidia and Apple, reported a record profit, up sixty-nine percent.

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
[andrew] On the cyber desk, a heads-up if your company uses Palo Alto Networks firewalls. Palo Alto says hackers have been exploiting a serious flaw in the management screen of its PAN-OS software. An attacker who already has a login and network access can take full control of the firewall. Palo Alto calls the attacks very limited so far, and it's released fixes. Its main advice is to make sure that management screen can only be reached from trusted internal addresses. CISA wants federal agencies patched by Saturday.
