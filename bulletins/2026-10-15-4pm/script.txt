## US News | sting=us
[ava] President Trump has canceled plans to livestream the firing-squad execution of Nidal Hasan, the Army doctor who killed thirteen people at Fort Hood in two thousand nine. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had said the December third execution would be broadcast online. Trump wrote on Truth Social that many good people would rather not watch him die, and that Hasan will still be executed as scheduled, but not streamed. The Pentagon says Trump and Hegseth spoke beforehand and are completely aligned. Lawmakers in both parties welcomed the change. An A.B.C. News Washington Post poll found about eight in ten Americans opposed livestreaming it. Critics had warned a public broadcast could look like authoritarian spectacle and risk turning Hasan into a martyr; some of Hegseth's religious allies had favored public executions.
[ava] Separately, the F.B.I. and Postal Inspection Service are investigating letters with an unknown powdery substance sent to election offices and secretaries of state, including in Louisiana, Oklahoma, and Ohio. Some offices evacuated. Officials urge anyone who receives suspicious mail to treat it as potentially hazardous. They have not identified what the substance is or who sent it.

## International | sting=world
[ava] In Ukraine, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy says Kyiv is done holding back from strikes on Russian oil refineries. He told the Financial Times he briefly paused those attacks to show readiness for talks, then resumed after Russia kept hitting Ukrainian energy sites and after limits on Western intelligence and Starlink access. Russia says Ukraine struck a refinery in the Ryazan region overnight, causing a fire. The Kremlin calls that a breach of the spirit of U.S. peace efforts. President Trump had unilaterally declared an energy ceasefire that neither side fully signed onto. Zelenskyy also says Ukraine is short of Patriot interceptor missiles. Britain's defense secretary, Wes Streeting, is in Washington stressing support for Ukraine.

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
[ava] In A.I., Amazon's Bee brand launched two new personal note-taking devices. The Bee Frontier is about seventy-five dollars and clips to a wrist or clothes. The Bee Eclipse is about a hundred dollars and magnets onto the back of a phone. Both listen to conversations, transcribe them, and turn them into summaries, calendar items, and to-dos. Amazon says raw audio is processed and deleted, with transcripts encrypted under keys the device controls. A paid Bee Pro plan unlocks Alexa and Amazon shopping hooks. Privacy advocates will watch how often people actually leave a mic on all day. Frontier is available now; Eclipse is coming soon.

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
[andrew] On cyber, Cisco says attackers are actively exploiting a critical flaw in Secure Email Gateway, tracked as C-V-E two thousand twenty-six dash seventy-six five one nine, with a nine-point-eight severity score. If the file-reputation service is on and the scanner tools are outdated, a crafted email attachment can let an unauthenticated attacker write files and run commands as root. Patches shipped in mid-September. CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list and wants federal agencies to patch by October seventeenth. Check logs beyond the appliance itself — attackers may try to cover their tracks.
[andrew] Also today: the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts says a cyber incident at BAE Systems has taken down the Bankruptcy Noticing Center. Federal bankruptcy notices nationwide are halted until systems are restored. BAE is investigating.

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
[ava] And finally, a seventeen-year-old who decided kids deserve bikes. Jonathan Thai of Alpharetta, Georgia, was training for triathlons when his local R.E.I. closed a community bike workshop that served low-income families. He founded the Pedal Forward Foundation, raised more than thirty thousand dollars, secured four hundred donated bikes, handed out five hundred helmets, and runs free clinics for thirty to forty-five kids at a time.
[ava] His parents grew up in rural postwar Vietnam, and he says a bike isn't just recreation — for a family without a car, it's how you get to school, the grocery store, and work. The foundation now has chapters in New York and Tennessee. Points of Light has already honored him. He's seventeen.
