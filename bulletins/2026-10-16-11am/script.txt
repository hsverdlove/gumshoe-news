## US News | sting=us
[ava] The Associated Press reports the U.S. is making an unprecedented attempt to bring three shuttered nuclear plants back online, driven by soaring electricity demand, much of it from data centers. The Energy Department is lending as much as four point four billion dollars to restart Palisades in Michigan, the Crane Clean Energy Center at Pennsylvania's Three Mile Island, and Duane Arnold in Iowa. Together they'd restore about two and a quarter gigawatts of power.
[ava] The timelines are long. Crane is aiming for twenty twenty-seven, and Duane Arnold, whose loan of up to one point nine billion dollars closed last month, is targeting early twenty twenty-nine, pending federal regulators' approval. Supporters see reliable, carbon-free power for a grid under strain. Skeptics question whether taxpayer-backed loans to revive aging plants are the best use of the money compared with building new capacity.
[ava] In Washington, the new White House press secretary, Katie Zacharia, is defending herself after a rocky first week. The A.P. says she told an interviewer she didn't have time to fully prepare for Tuesday's first briefing, and that keeping it short was always the plan because the president was taking questions himself later that morning. And President Trump's rally with Georgia Republicans is set for Dalton later today.

## International | sting=world
[ava] Overseas. At a European Union summit in Brussels this morning, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy asked European leaders for at least seventy billion euros more next year for defense, weapons and resilience, according to Reuters. He criticized President Trump's deal to buy Russian diesel, saying it puts money, quote, straight into Putin's pocket, and called Moscow's stance on talks a parody of peace negotiations. He also said the Starshield satellite service Ukraine relies on is better, but not fully restored.
[ava] The other side: Trump has blamed Ukraine's strikes on Russian refineries for pushing up world oil prices, calling them a disgrace in a Newsmax interview, and a U.S. official denied that the White House ordered any slowdown of Starshield.
[ava] In the occupied West Bank, Reuters witnesses say about eighty masked Israeli settlers attacked Palestinian villagers harvesting olives in Abu Falah, near Ramallah, burning trees and throwing rocks. Among the injured was Heidi Levine, an American photojournalist working for The Washington Post. She told reporters from her hospital bed that she was knocked down and kicked repeatedly, and that her flak jacket and helmet probably saved her life. She's in stable condition. The Israeli military had not commented as of the Reuters report. Prime Minister Netanyahu has criticized settler violence in the past, but critics, including some of Israel's allies, say his government has done little to stop it.

## Technology | sting=tech
[andrew] On the tech desk: OpenAI updated its ChatGPT extension for Chrome. The Verge and Engadget report it can now read your open tabs, and in a mode called Work, it can act in them: fill out forms, send messages, download files and organize tabs.
[andrew] It's handy, but security folks have a fair caution. An agent that can act inside your logged-in accounts can also be tricked by instructions hidden in a web page it reads. A sensible start is a separate browser profile, with banking and email kept out of it.

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
[ava] In A.I., Microsoft wants a frontier model of its own. Microsoft A.I. says it has finished pre-training a model called MAI Base Two, with two point eight trillion total parameters, trained on sixteen thousand of Nvidia's top G.B. two hundred chips over thirty-eight days. Microsoft claims it's four to twelve times more compute-efficient than DeepSeek's latest base model.
[ava] Those efficiency numbers are Microsoft's own, there are no independent benchmarks yet, and the model is still being fine-tuned, so nothing ships today. But it's a signal that Microsoft doesn't intend to rely on OpenAI alone.

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
[andrew] Cyber desk, and a deadline. Federal agencies have until tomorrow to fix four actively exploited flaws CISA added to its must-patch list: one in Palo Alto Networks firewalls, one in Cisco's Secure Email Gateway that lets attackers run code as root, and two in the AhsayCBS backup server. Huntress says attackers chained the Ahsay bugs to install crypto-mining software. If your company runs any of these, the deadline isn't just for the government.

## Local News & Events | sting=local
[ava] Closer to home. New Hampshire has asked the U.S. Supreme Court to pause a judge's ruling on student IDs before the November third election. Federal Judge Joseph Laplante left in place the state law dropping student IDs from the list of accepted voter identification, but ruled that election officials may still consider a student ID along with other evidence of who someone is. Secretary of State David Scanlan and Attorney General John Formella argue that changing instructions this close to Election Day will confuse voters and poll workers. The group that sued says the ruling simply removes a barrier for eligible voters. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson asked for a response by Monday morning.
[ava] Flu season is here early. N.H.P.R. reports flu cases started showing up in September instead of October or November, and Southern New Hampshire Medical Center has already seen nineteen cases at its urgent care sites. Doctors say get the shot now, since it takes about two weeks to build protection.
