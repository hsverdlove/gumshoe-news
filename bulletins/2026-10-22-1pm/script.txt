## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Lucas case update
[ava] We start with more on this morning's top story. At a news conference in Norfolk, Attorney General Todd Blanche and F.B.I. Director Kash Patel laid out the federal case against Virginia State Senator L. Louise Lucas. The unsealed indictment includes racketeering, marijuana distribution, money laundering and bribery-related fraud counts, and accuses Lucas, her relatives and associates of running what Patel called the Lucas Enterprise, using her chairmanship of the Senate's budget committee to shield the business and shape legislation. Blanche noted the investigation began before the current administration and said, quote, we don't do political paybacks.
[ava] Lucas answered on social media. She didn't address the specific charges, but said she grew up under Jim Crow and, quote, I will not be intimidated, I will not be bullied, and I will not back down. Her defense fund's lawyer called it a circus. Critics point to the timing, twelve days before the midterms, and her role leading Virginia Democrats' failed redistricting push; prosecutors say the facts, not politics, drove the case. She was due in federal court this afternoon.
// Story 2: Border heat ray
[ava] The Pentagon has cleared Homeland Security to test a military Active Denial System, often called a heat ray, at the southern border. According to a Defense Department memo reported by Military dot com and Axios, Customs and Border Protection gets one unit for a seven-month trial, and would operate it itself. The system fires a millimeter-wave beam that causes an intense but temporary burning sensation, meant to make people move away. The memo doesn't say where or under what rules it would be used. Supporters call it a nonlethal alternative to force; critics have questioned its safety for years, and how it would be used on migrants.

## International | sting=world
// Story 3: IRGC offensive doctrine
[ava] Overseas, Iran's Revolutionary Guard says its military doctrine has shifted from defensive to what it calls offensive and asymmetrical. Spokesman Hossein Mohebi said on state TV that if a country attacks Iran, Iran will not only respond but, quote, invade. Another Guard official warned that undersea internet cables in the Strait of Hormuz could be targeted if Iranian infrastructure is hit. Analysts note those cables mostly serve the Gulf states, which have backup land routes, so the global internet wouldn't go down. Meanwhile, U.S. media report dozens of Israeli jets ran a training exercise near Iran's western border, and Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, says Tehran will give diplomacy another chance only with strong evidence the U.S. is sincere.
// Story 4: US-Russia thaw analysis
[ava] And on Russia, a New York Times analysis argues Moscow is getting closer to normal relations with Washington without giving ground in Ukraine. It points to the diesel deal that eased sanctions on Russian fuel, U.S. pressure on Kyiv to stop striking Russian refineries, and a pause in new sanctions. Ukraine and many Europeans see that as rewarding Moscow. The administration's envoys argue the opposite: that economic engagement is the way to restart talks, and lower-level talks among the U.S., Russia and Ukraine are set for next month.

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 8: PingCAP TiDB breach
[ava] Cyber desk. If your company uses TiDB Cloud, the database service from PingCAP, check in with your team. PingCAP says attackers used stolen service-account credentials to get into its internal systems from September ninth to October seventh, exposing account and billing details, saved SQL editor content and some source code. It says customer database data was not affected and the stolen credentials are revoked. A group calling itself FulcrumSec claims it took over ten terabytes tied to a hundred eighty thousand organizations; that's unverified. The advice: rotate TiDB Cloud API keys and review who has access.
> source: PingCAP, "October 2026 Security Incident Report: Unauthorized Access to PingCAP Cloud Resources" (Oct 2026); Dark Reading, "TiDB Breach Exposes Flawed Cloud Security Assumptions" (Nate Nelson, Oct 22); Crypto Briefing (Oct 2026)

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
[ava] And finally, the raccoon people. Nine years ago, veterinarian Paul Bookbinder and his wife Sarah took in two injured baby raccoons but couldn't find a rehabber in time, and the kits died. They vowed it wouldn't happen again. They bought fifty-three acres in Earlville, New York, opened a raccoon rehab center, and have since helped more than eight hundred. Sarah once went through two hundred bottles a day bottle-feeding kits. Before release, the raccoons forage in sheds stocked with buried nuts and crickets, and every one gets its name engraved on a rock in a garden. Sarah jokes her mother has accepted that her grandchildren are mostly raccoons. Today they release the last six of the year: Malbec, Merlot, Ginger, Kim Chee, Syrah and Wasabi.
> source: The Washington Post, "Meet the couple dedicated to saving raccoons — hundreds of them" (Kyle Melnick, Oct 22)
