## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Hutchinson no-bill
[ava] In Washington, the Associated Press and NBC News report that a federal grand jury refused to indict Cassidy Hutchinson, the former White House aide who was the January sixth committee's star witness. Prosecutors were reportedly seeking a false-statements charge over her twenty twenty-two testimony about President Trump's actions that day. Grand juries rarely turn down federal prosecutors. Her testimony has long been disputed by Trump allies, while her supporters saw the case as political. A no-bill doesn't stop the department from trying again with another grand jury.
// Story 2: Essayli ruling
[ava] In Los Angeles, Judge J. Michael Seabright ruled that Bill Essayli has been unlawfully serving as the region's top federal prosecutor, and barred him from two criminal cases. The ruling is on hold while the Justice Department appeals, so Essayli is still running the office. Courts have split on how long the administration can keep acting prosecutors in place without Senate confirmation.
// Story 3: Canada tariffs
[ava] And on trade, the White House is dropping its fifty percent tariffs on some Canadian goods, including farm machinery, refrigeration equipment, plywood and seaweed products, starting Friday, while adding tariffs on others, such as certain valves and carpets. The Wall Street Journal reports the move followed requests from Maine Senator Susan Collins on behalf of Maine businesses, a week before her re-election race.
// California update
[ava] One follow-up: Judge David Carter's written order in the California ballot case is still expected today.

## International | sting=world
// Story 4: Spain crash update
[ava] Overseas, the toll from this morning's high-speed train crash in Spain has risen sharply. Castilla-La Mancha's regional government now says at least sixty-four people were killed and more than a hundred injured when a Renfe train hit the back of a stopped Iryo train near Urda, south of Madrid. The regional president warns more victims may be found in the worst-hit Iryo cars. The government's first hypothesis is human error, but officials stress the investigation will decide. Most Spanish parties have suspended campaign events.

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 8: NY chatbot law
[andrew] In A.I., New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed a law limiting A.I. chatbot features for kids. Chatbots can't pretend to be human with users under eighteen, and must avoid things like flattery, unprompted emotional questions, and encouraging kids to keep secrets. Companies also have to verify ages, with at least one option that doesn't rely on a government I.D. Child-safety advocates call it overdue; critics of age-check laws generally argue they force companies to collect more personal data, and that a patchwork of state rules is hard to follow.
> source: The Verge, "NY Governor Kathy Hochul signs law banning harmful AI chatbot features for kids" (Oct 28)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 9: Oracle EBS and Skyw4lker
[ava] Cyber desk. Attackers are already scanning the internet for Oracle E-Business Suite systems that haven't installed last week's patch for a critical flaw that lets someone take over a server without logging in. Oracle hasn't confirmed attacks, but the threat-intel firm Defused saw exploit attempts Tuesday. Oracle's business software has been a favorite target of extortion gangs.
[ava] And researchers at ANY dot RUN describe a phishing campaign called Skyw4lker aimed mostly at U.S. manufacturers, tech firms and health care. It sends a fake contract that leads to a real Microsoft sign-in page and tricks people into approving the attacker's device. The tip: never enter a code from an email into a Microsoft sign-in prompt unless you started that sign-in yourself.
> source: BleepingComputer, "Hackers now scanning systems for recently-patched Oracle flaw" (Oct 28); The Hacker News on Oracle EBS probing (Oct 28); The Hacker News, "Skyw4lker Device Code Phishing Campaign Exposed" (Oct 28), citing ANY.RUN

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
[ava] And finally, from Cape Cod. Retired tech executive John Cooper of Sandwich volunteers with the American Cancer Society's Road to Recovery program, giving free rides to treatment. For six weeks he picked up Kerry Sullivan every weekday and drove her to radiation in Plymouth. She told Boston twenty-five News that what could have been a depressing six weeks became, in her words, the bright spot of her day. Cooper says it's not like an Uber ride; if a patient arrives smiling, that's an epic win. Last year, more than six thousand volunteers gave nearly ninety thousand rides across Massachusetts.
