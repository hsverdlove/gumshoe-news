## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Salon shuts down
[ava] Story one. Salon, one of the web's pioneering online magazines, announced yesterday that it is ceasing publication after thirty-one years. The San Francisco-based outlet launched in November nineteen ninety-five, when the commercial web was still young, and helped invent a voice-driven form of digital journalism that mixed politics, culture and criticism. In a goodbye note, the staff said its independent publishing model is no longer sustainable after profound changes in the business, though the archive is expected to remain available for now. Admirers mourned a distinctive early-internet brand; longtime critics of the site's later years called the end overdue. Either way, another founding-era web publication is gone.
> source: Salon goodbye note (Nov 5); AP News (Nov 5); The Verge (Nov 5); Deadline (Nov 5)

## International | sting=world
// Story 4: Irish media bomb threats
[ava] Story four, Ireland. This morning, R T E and several other Irish newsrooms evacuated after identical emailed bomb threats. Gardaí searched the Donnybrook campus with sniffer dogs; R T E switched much of its schedule to pre-recorded programs and kept one radio show on air from a mobile unit. The Irish Independent, Newstalk and Today F M also cleared offices for a time. Taoiseach Micheál Martin called the threats worrying and said authorities will pursue whoever is behind them, especially with a European summit in Dublin next week. Officials link the emails to a wave of hoax bomb threats that disrupted at least two dozen European flights yesterday. No explosives have been reported found.
> source: RTÉ News (Nov 6); BBC News (Nov 6); The Irish Times (Nov 6); The Journal.ie live updates (Nov 6)
// Story 5: Paraguay president resigns
[ava] Story five, Paraguay. President Santiago Peña announced his resignation on Thursday amid a crisis at digital lender Ueno Bank, whose solvency collapse froze access for roughly three million depositors. Peña said he was stepping aside to protect national stability; Congress instead advanced impeachment proceedings, so the political outcome is still unsettled. Vice President Pedro Alliana would take over if Peña leaves office. Supporters cast the move as putting the country first; critics say regulators and political leaders ignored warning signs at a lightly supervised digital bank.
> source: BBC News (Nov 5–6); The Guardian (Nov 5); AP News (Nov 5); ABC News Australia (Nov 6)

## Technology | sting=tech
// Story 7: FAA floppy disks
[andrew] Story seven. The Federal Aviation Administration says it will finally retire floppy disks from air traffic control by the end of twenty twenty-eight. Controllers at more than four hundred facilities still get weather and other critical updates from systems dating to the nineteen eighties that rely on those disks. The replacement is called the Enterprise Information Display System, which puts alerts, airspace restrictions, pilot reports and notices to airmen on one modern screen. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy framed it as overdue modernization. Controllers, one suspects, will be glad to stop hunting for a working three-and-a-half-inch disk.
> source: FAA newsroom release (Nov 5); Reuters (Nov 5); The Verge (Nov 5); Engadget (Nov 5)

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 8: GPT-6 Cyber
[andrew] Story eight. OpenAI launched GPT-6 Cyber, a model built to find and validate software flaws while staying inside the scope researchers set. In one campaign it helped develop working local privilege-escalation exploits for nine hundred forty-eight Linux kernel vulnerabilities and uncovered eighty-one previously unknown high-severity issues in Firefox, which Mozilla is patching. OpenAI rates the model Critical under its preparedness framework and is rolling it out first to approved Daybreak Red security customers, with stronger misuse monitors aimed at critical infrastructure. Defenders call it a force multiplier; critics worry the same capabilities could speed up attackers if access controls fail.
> source: OpenAI, Introducing GPT-6 Cyber (Nov 5); OpenAI, A thousand fewer paths to root (Nov 5); Axios (Nov 5); TechRadar Pro (Nov 5–6); SecurityWeek (Nov 5)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 9: Refrigeration attacks
[andrew] Story nine. The F B I and CISA, joined by Health and Human Services, Agriculture and Transportation, warn that hackers are actively targeting industrial refrigeration and cold-chain controllers worldwide. Attackers hit unpatched or internet-exposed temperature systems, sometimes forcing continuous defrost or heating cycles that spoil food, damage equipment and create fire risk. Recent waves hit hundreds of European grocery stores and even altered settings at French blood-storage sites. The advice from the agencies: pull controllers off the public internet, change default passwords, patch promptly, and isolate those systems from office networks.
> source: BleepingComputer, Lawrence Abrams (Nov 5, 7:56 PM ET), citing joint FBI/CISA/HHS/USDA/DOT advisory

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
// Story 12: ISS solar arrays complete
[ava] Story twelve, something good. On the International Space Station, astronauts Joshua Kutryk and Anil Menon finished a five-year project to add eight new roll-out solar arrays. Thursday's nearly seven-hour spacewalk unfurled the last sixty-three-foot wing after a brief power-connector snag that Mission Control worked through in orbital night. NASA says the eight arrays can boost available power by up to thirty percent and help keep the station running through its planned retirement around twenty thirty. Menon called it a privilege of a lifetime — a staircase, he said, toward the moon and eventually Mars.
> source: Spaceflight Now, Houston Harwood (Nov 5); NASA Expedition 75 spacewalk coverage (Nov 5)
