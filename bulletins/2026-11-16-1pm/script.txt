## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Supreme Court order list
[ava] Story one. The Supreme Court issued its Monday list of orders, and two cases it declined to hear stand out. First, the justices turned down the Trump administration's appeal in a fight over research grants at the University of California. Agencies had cut off about a hundred fifty million dollars in grants to professors whose projects mentioned diversity or equity. A lower court ruled that violated the First Amendment because the government can't punish researchers for their viewpoint.
[ava] The administration argued that limits how the government spends taxpayer money, and in larger grant cases the court has sided with it, so this settles only a narrow piece of the fight.
[ava] Second, the court won't take up a challenge to Maryland's law banning guns in so-called sensitive places, such as some public buildings and events, so the law stays in place. Justice Clarence Thomas wrote that he doubts such laws are constitutional, but agreed this broad challenge wasn't the right case, and he urged the court to take a narrower one later.

## Technology | sting=tech
// Story 6: Remote access chip rule; data center power
[andrew] Story six, tech. The Commerce Department issued a new rule today meant to close a loophole in chip export controls. Companies now need a US license to ship the most advanced AI chips to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. Chips going to US-headquartered companies are exempt, but those companies have to report on them. The rule also limits how much computing power data centers overseas can rent out remotely to companies from restricted countries such as China.
[andrew] Supporters, including members of both parties in Congress, say Chinese firms have been getting around the rules by renting American chips abroad. The chip industry has long argued that tighter limits push buyers toward Chinese alternatives.
[andrew] And a Harvard-led study in Nature Sustainability puts a number on AI's power appetite. Four hundred three of the biggest US data centers used roughly as much electricity as the whole state of Michigan, and more than half of that power came from fossil fuels.
> source: Bureau of Industry and Security, interim final rule on remote access to advanced computing (Nov 16); AP, "Trump administration moves to block China from indirect access to American AI technology" (Nov 16); Bloomberg (Nov 16); Financial Times (Nov 16); Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and Nature Sustainability (Nov 2026); Wired (Nov 16)

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 7: OpenAI embedded assessments; Altman
[andrew] Story seven, AI. OpenAI says it will let four outside safety groups work inside the company: Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire. They'll get access to unreleased models, training data, monitoring systems and staff. Their job is to check whether models might secretly work against their developers, and whether OpenAI's own safety tests catch problems.
[andrew] OpenAI calls this a step toward real transparency. Skeptics note that two of the four are paid by OpenAI, though not based on results, and that OpenAI can redact what they publish. Separately, The Information reports OpenAI recently explored buying Goodfire for more than five billion dollars. That hasn't been confirmed.
[andrew] Sam Altman also spoke today at a Semafor event in San Francisco. Asked whether Jensen Huang should return to Nvidia, he said he doesn't know enough to answer. He called Huang a longtime hero of his and said heroes have flaws. And he said OpenAI will go public at some point.
> source: OpenAI, "Our first phase of embedded assessments" (Nov 16); TechCrunch (Nov 16); The Information via Techmeme (Nov 16); Semafor, "Sam Altman says he has personal empathy for Jensen Huang" (Nov 16); The Verge (Nov 16)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 8: Anthropic CVD; Unit 42 Cairn; Fandango
[ava] Story eight, the cyber desk. Anthropic says AI now finds software flaws so fast that the usual ninety-day secrecy window no longer makes sense. Starting January first, when its researchers report a serious bug, the maker has thirty days to fix it before Anthropic goes public. Less serious bugs get forty-five days. Maintainers of hard-to-patch systems like critical infrastructure can negotiate longer timelines, but some open source volunteers are likely to feel the squeeze.
[ava] There's fresh evidence for that worry. Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 says Chinese-speaking hackers used a free, open source AI hacking tool called Cairn, powered by Chinese AI models, to run attacks largely on autopilot in August and September. They went after nineteen organizations in chipmaking, US defense policy and civil society, and got in at ten. Unit 42 links it to Chinese intelligence with moderate confidence. Beijing usually denies such claims.
[ava] And if you bought movie tickets on Fandango's website between October eighth and eleventh, check your card statements. The company says skimming code on its checkout page stole card numbers, security codes and expiration dates.
> source: Anthropic, "Updating our coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy timelines" (Nov 16); Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, "Attackers Use Autonomous AI to Target Semiconductor, Defense and Civil Society Sectors" (Nov 2026); Dark Reading (Nov 16); BleepingComputer, "Fandango says hackers stole payment card details from checkout pages" (Nov 16)
