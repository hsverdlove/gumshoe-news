## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Florida DMV outage; patching likely behind outages
[ava] Story one. Add another system to the list of recent tech outages. Florida's Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles says its statewide driver license and vehicle system, called ORION, suffered what it calls a degradation of service today. County tax collector offices and state service centers use it to process licenses, registrations, titles and plates, so many Floridians could not finish those transactions. Around one in the afternoon the agency said it had identified the problem and was working to restore full service, but it gave no timeline. So far there's no sign of a cyberattack.
[ava] So what is behind the string of outages, from the FAA to the banks and Microsoft? Reuters reports a growing view among security experts and US officials that a surge of rushed software patches is a big factor. AI tools now find software flaws faster and more cheaply than ever, and companies are racing to fix them. Security firm Qualys says Microsoft alone has patched more than twelve hundred vulnerabilities this month, nearly twenty times as many as a year ago. Experts say emergency fixes often go into core systems within hours, without full testing. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said this week that patching is at times creating short-term disruption. That's still an emerging explanation, not a final finding, and officials say the individual outages do not appear to be connected.

## International | sting=world

// Story 3: US-Venezuela military ties
[ava] Story three. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth says the United States and Venezuela have signed an agreement to restore military ties, from joint training to intelligence sharing. He announced it during a visit to Caracas, where he met interim president Delcy Rodríguez. It's the latest sign of Washington's deepening influence there since US forces ousted Nicolás Maduro in January. Hegseth said Venezuela has gone from the region's most aggressive threat to a compliant security partner, and pointed to joint work against drug trafficking. Rodríguez said she hopes the cooperation will help address common threats. Critics of the policy have questioned how much say Venezuelans themselves have had in the country's direction under an interim government closely tied to Washington.

[andrew] Story six, AI. Google DeepMind is opening up Genie 4, its newest world model, to subscribers of its top-tier AI Ultra plan. ## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 7: nginx2root; Wellington IT credit unions
[ava] Story seven, the cyber desk. If you run web servers, this one matters. Researchers at Rapid7 and the vendor F5 today disclosed a critical flaw in NGINX, one of the most widely used web servers on the internet. It's been dubbed nginx two root. The bug sits in how NGINX handles HTTP two traffic and dates back about ten years, to version one point nine point five. In some cases it lets an attacker with no login run code on the server. Rapid7 has a working proof of concept, but says it takes roughly a hundred twenty-eight gigabytes of traffic on a fast connection and specific hardware, so mass attacks are harder. Fixes are out in versions one point thirty-one point nine and one point thirty point seven. If you can't patch right away, turning off HTTP two blocks it.
[ava] And in Europe, a reported hack of Belfast-based software supplier Wellington IT exposed saved payee details for members of about forty Irish credit unions, including names and bank account numbers. Passwords, PINs and card details were not affected, but members are warned to watch for phishing.
> source: Rapid7, "CVE-2026-123007: Critical NGINX HTTP/2 Integer Underflow Leads to RCE" (Nov 18); nginx security advisories (Nov 18); The Hacker News (Nov 18); BleepingComputer, "Data breach impacts 40 Irish credit unions in reported IT provider hack" (Nov 18); RTE and Irish Examiner via BleepingComputer

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
// Story 10: Santa Clara Valley liveforever
[ava] And finally, story ten. Not far from Silicon Valley grows a wildflower found nowhere else on Earth. The Santa Clara Valley dudleya, nicknamed the liveforever, is a succulent that can look shriveled and dead for months, then plump right back up after even a little rain and burst into pale yellow flowers. It's federally endangered and one of the most poached wild plants in California. Now Santa Clara University biologist Justen Whittall and his students are collecting its tiny seeds, picking the most genetically diverse ones, and breeding plants to return to the wild. A colleague, Stephen McCabe, took a different tack. He bred colorful hybrids to sell to nurseries, hoping to flood the market so poachers have no one to sell to. As Whittall put it, at minimum they want to prevent extinction, and at best improve its odds. In other words, they want to make sure the liveforever lives forever.
> source: Good News Network, "Students Fighting to Save Poached Plant Species That Only Exists Near Their Campus" (Nov 18), citing CBS News Bay Area
