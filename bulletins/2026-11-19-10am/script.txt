## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Mullin TV timeout
[ava] Story one. Politico reports that new White House chief of staff Stephen Miller has pulled Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin off television. According to two people familiar with it, officials were frustrated with Mullin's messaging on the recent string of outages, including a Fox News interview Monday where he cited a tech friend who thought the outages might be related to AI. Officials were also unhappy that he sounded open to some kind of immigration deal. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton and Secretary of State Marco Rubio have taken over as the main voices on the outages. A White House official framed the move as Miller doing his job, getting the cabinet coordinated on one message. The White House and Homeland Security both repeat that there is no sign of a foreign cyberattack behind the outages. Mullin has not commented on the report.

// Story 2: Russian agent targeted Palantir workers
[ava] Story two. A federal grand jury in California has indicted a Russian national, Igor D'iu, on charges of acting as an unregistered agent of Russia and stalking employees of a Silicon Valley defense technology company. NBC News and Bloomberg identify the company as Palantir, whose software helps Ukraine's military. Prosecutors say D'iu was recruited over Telegram, crossed the Mexican border illegally this summer, and was told to photograph employees' laptop screens at a coffee shop near the company's Palo Alto office. They say he later tracked workers' home addresses, and in September followed one woman for nearly thirty miles until she drove to a San Francisco police station. D'iu has been in custody since early November and is due back in court Monday. These are allegations, and he has not yet entered a plea.
> source: US Department of Justice, "Russian National Indicted for Acting as a Foreign Agent of Russia to Target Silicon Valley Technology and Defense Company" (Nov 18); NBC News, "Russian man accused of spying targeted Palantir workers, sources say" (Nov 18); Bloomberg Law (Nov 18); Los Angeles Times (Nov 18)

## International | sting=world
// Story 3: Putin rules out talks after Crimean Bridge strikes
[ava] Story three, the world. Hours after this morning's three-way summit with President Trump and Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin said Russia will not negotiate after Ukraine's strikes on the Crimean Bridge. Putin told reporters in Shenzhen he had asked his Security Council to consider a partial halt to attacks on energy and shipping, but that the bridge strikes ended that idea. He promised a response he said would be far more painful, and vowed to rebuild the bridge. Ukraine has hit the bridge three times in the past week, dropping a road span into the sea, and struck the rail line again overnight. Putin called the bridge a purely civilian structure. Ukraine's military says it is Russia's main route for moving fuel, ammunition and troops into Crimea and southern Ukraine.
> source: Anadolu Agency, "Putin says Russia will not negotiate ceasefire after Ukraine inflicted 'serious damage' on Crimean Bridge" (Nov 19); Meduza citing TASS (Nov 19); Kyiv Post (Nov 19); Ukrainska Pravda (Nov 19)

// Story 4: ICJ Myanmar genocide ruling
[ava] Story four. For the first time, the United Nations' top court has found a country responsible for genocide. The International Court of Justice ruled this morning that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya Muslim minority during a twenty seventeen military campaign that drove hundreds of thousands into Bangladesh. The case was brought by Gambia. The court ordered Myanmar to allow refugees to return safely, punish those responsible and pay compensation. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said Myanmar must comply. Britain is expelling Myanmar's defense attaché and calling for a Security Council meeting. Myanmar's military government has long denied genocide, saying it was fighting insurgents, and the court has no police of its own to enforce the ruling. Rohingya refugees welcomed the verdict, but many doubt they'll be going home soon.
> source: AP, "For the first time, UN's highest court finds a country responsible for genocide: Myanmar" (Nov 19); Reuters via The Straits Times, "Reactions as top UN court finds Myanmar guilty of genocide against Rohingya" (Nov 19); UK Foreign Office statement (Nov 19); Anadolu Agency (Nov 19)

## Technology | sting=tech
// Story 6: TSMC halts advanced orders for Chinese designers
[andrew] Story six, tech. New Commerce Department rules took effect today, and Reuters reports that Taiwan's TSMC is responding by halting orders from affected Chinese chip designers for its sixteen nanometer and smaller processes unless they get US export licenses. Most cutting-edge AI chips are made at seven nanometers or smaller. Chinese customers will generally be limited to older twenty-eight and forty nanometer technology, and only for chips without high-bandwidth memory or advanced packaging. Until now, TSMC could check a design itself to decide whether it fell under the controls. That route is gone. Supporters say it closes one of the last loopholes for building powerful AI chips for China. Critics warn it could speed China's push to build its own chipmaking and squeeze Taiwanese and Korean sales.
> source: Reuters via Investing.com, "TSMC tightens China AI chip curbs as new US rules close manufacturing loophole, sources say" (Nov 19); Federal Register, "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors" (Nov 19)

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 7: Banks shift AI agent liability to customers
[andrew] Story seven, AI. If you've thought about letting an AI assistant into your bank account, read the fine print first. Citibank changed its online agreement this week. It says customers who give a third-party AI agent access are responsible for whatever it does, even transactions that go beyond their instructions, and even if the AI tool is hacked or leaks their password. TechCrunch reports PNC and Citizens have made similar changes, following JPMorgan and Bank of America last month. Citi says it wants customers to understand the risks. Critics note banks also see AI agents as a competitive threat, since they could help people avoid fees, and Citi's own AI tools are exempt. Consumer lawyers say federal rules may still protect you if a transfer is truly unauthorized, so report anything suspicious right away.
> source: TechCrunch, "More banks say customers using AI agents are on their own — even if they get hacked" (Nov 18); Citi statement to TechCrunch (Nov 18); Edit USA, "Citi AI-agent terms and Wells Fargo's Nov. 24 rules" (Nov 2026); The Neuron daily digest (Nov 19)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 8: JPMorgan vendor breach; Edge Lockdown Mode
[ava] Story eight, the cyber desk. JPMorgan Chase says hackers broke into outside companies that provide services to the bank. The bank believes the attackers got marketing data that may include some customers' names and email addresses, and it will contact those affected directly. JPMorgan says this has nothing to do with the transaction glitches some customers saw earlier this week. If you bank with Chase, watch for phishing emails that use your name.
[ava] And Microsoft is testing a Lockdown Mode for its Edge browser, modeled on Apple's feature of the same name. It switches off the graphics interfaces that websites use for 3D games and maps, a favorite path for attacks that try to break out of the browser. Microsoft estimates it would have blocked about sixty percent of privilege escalation attacks on Edge users. It's aimed at journalists, executives and others likely to be targeted, and it's in the Canary test version for now.
> source: Reuters via StreetInsider, "JPMorgan says third-party vendors targeted in cyberattack" (Nov 19); LiveNOW from FOX (Nov 19); BleepingComputer, "Microsoft tests new Edge Lockdown Mode that disables GPU APIs" (Nov 19)
