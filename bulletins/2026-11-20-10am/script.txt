## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Senate passes DEATH BETS Act
[ava] Story one. The Senate has passed a bipartisan bill to ban prediction markets from offering bets on war, terrorism, assassinations or a person's death. It passed last night by voice vote, with no recorded opposition. The bill is called the Death Bets Act, and it's led by Democrat Adam Schiff and Republican John Curtis. It would bar federally regulated exchanges such as Kalshi and Polymarket from listing those contracts, including bets on US military operations. Schiff says the markets create rampant opportunities for insider trading and put national security at risk. He points to six Polymarket accounts that reportedly made about one point two million dollars betting on the timing of US strikes on Iran in February. Their owners were never identified. Regulators can already block most of these contracts, so supporters say the bill mainly writes that ban into law. Some traders and forecasters argue such markets can surface useful information. The bill now goes to the House.

// Story 2: DOJ drops Alaska Senate challenge
[ava] Story two. The Justice Department is giving up its lawsuit over Alaska's Senate race. Before the election, the department sued to potentially block certification if Republican Dan Sullivan lost. It argued that a second candidate also named Dan Sullivan on the ballot was confusing voters. A federal judge threw the case out on November first, and Democrat Mary Peltola went on to win with more than half the vote. Last night the department told the appeals court the case is now moot. It said it reluctantly acknowledges there's no way to tell whether votes for the other Dan Sullivan were meant for the senator. Because Peltola won an outright majority, those votes didn't affect the result. Critics, including Alaska's Republican-led state government, had called the lawsuit meritless. Supporters of the challenge still say the second candidate was a deliberate spoiler, which Peltola and that candidate deny. Peltola says that for Alaskans there is only one direction, forward.
> source: AP, "Trump administration moves to have lawsuit dismissed in challenge to Alaska's US Senate race" (Nov 19-20); NBC News, "DOJ seeks dismissal of its lawsuit in the Alaska Senate race" (Nov 19); ABC News (Nov 19); The Hill (Nov 19); Washington Post (Nov 19)

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 7: Meta Muse privacy mishaps
[andrew] Story seven, AI. Reuters reviewed internal Meta posts showing problems with its Muse AI agent, one of the fastest-growing apps ever. Employees testing it reported that Muse sent them messages about childcare requests they never made and orders they never placed. In one case it gave out bank balances that weren't theirs. One worker said Muse reminded her to answer a Signal message from her sister, though she never connected Signal. Working with Signal and the AI Now Institute, Reuters reproduced that on an Android phone and a Mac. Muse used the notification and accessibility permissions granted at setup. The same test was blocked on an iPhone, and Apple has since closed the Mac loophole. Meta calls these isolated incidents. It says many were hallucinations that have been fixed, and that Muse only reaches apps people allow. Signal's president called the rollout technologically reckless. If you try one of these agents, check its permissions carefully.
> source: Reuters via Investing.com, "As Meta bets big on Muse AI agent, early safety mishaps surface" (Nov 18); TechCrunch (Nov 19); The Verge (Nov 19); Ars Technica (Nov 2026)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 8: Cairncross out, Davies in; EU Commission ARTEX hack
[ava] Story eight, the cyber desk. The White House has replaced its national cyber director after a string of big technology outages. Sean Cairncross is out. Pentagon chief information officer Kirsten Davies will serve as acting director. A permanent pick would need Senate confirmation. Axios reports chief of staff Stephen Miller pushed for the change, and one official said Cairncross lacked strong cyber credentials. The White House still says there's no sign the outages were a coordinated or foreign cyberattack. Davies says the goal is to build resilience into the systems the country runs on.
[ava] In Europe, the European Commission confirmed that hackers used an AI tool in an attack detected October twenty-second. It knocked out staff payroll, leave and medical claims systems for more than ten days. Investigators say the attackers used ARTEX, a free AI hacking tool. Last week the FBI warned that criminals and state hackers are using it widely. The Commission says it has found no broad data breach and no ransom demand.
> source: CNBC, "White House replaces cyber director Sean Cairncross amid widespread tech outages" (Nov 20); Axios, "Trump names Kirsten Davies as new national cyber director" (Nov 19); UPI (Nov 20); The Record, "European Commission confirms cyberattack, says hackers used AI tool" (Nov 19); Euronews (Nov 19); CERT-EU blog on ARTEX

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
// Story 11: Deirdre Keane seven marathons while pregnant
[ava] And finally, story eleven. Deirdre Keane, a thirty-eight-year-old nurse practitioner from New York, planned twenty twenty-six as her last hurrah before starting a family. She signed up for all seven of the world's major marathons. Then, after Boston and London in April, she found out she was pregnant. With her doctor's approval, she kept going at an easy pace, with walking as a backup plan. She ran Sydney at twenty-five weeks, Berlin at twenty-nine and Chicago at thirty-one. She finished the New York City Marathon at thirty-four weeks in just over four hours. The course passed a block from the hospital where she'll deliver, and yes, there were jokes. Her father, an Irish immigrant, ran marathons too, and died of cancer when she was thirteen. She says she wants to teach her son what he taught her, that he can do hard things.
