## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Palisades Fire verdict
[ava] Story one. A federal jury in Los Angeles has convicted Jonathan Rinderknecht of starting the fire that became last year's Palisades Fire. That blaze killed twelve people and destroyed thousands of homes in Los Angeles and Malibu, the most destructive fire in the city's history. Prosecutors said the thirty-year-old former Uber driver lit a small brush fire just after midnight on New Year's Day twenty twenty-five. Firefighters put it out, but it kept smoldering underground and flared back up six days later.
[ava] This was a retrial. The first jury in June deadlocked ten to two in favor of acquittal. This time jurors deliberated about two days and convicted him on both counts. One juror said the turning point was Rinderknecht's own testimony that he was on the hill that night and saw no fireworks. The defense had pointed to fireworks as another possible cause, and says it will appeal. Sentencing is set for March seventeenth. He faces five to forty years.

// Story 2: Executive order on contractors and Congress
[ava] Story two. Late Friday, President Trump signed an executive order telling companies with federal contracts to alert the White House before sharing information with Congress, and to hold back anything the administration deems confidential. Contractors that don't comply could lose their contracts. It follows a directive this week from White House Counsel Will Scharf requiring agency employees to get White House approval before agreeing to testify.
[ava] The White House says this protects sensitive communications, including national security matters, and keeps contractors from accidentally exposing privileged material. Critics see it as an attempt to blunt the investigations Democrats have promised once they take control of both chambers in January. Legal experts say it could end up in court, especially if a contractor is told to ignore a congressional subpoena.
> source: CNBC/Reuters, "Trump orders federal contractors to notify White House before giving Congress information" (Nov 21); Hindustan Times/Reuters (Nov 21)

## International | sting=world
// Story 3: Sparta II hit in Gulf of Finland; Crimean Bridge update; Kryvyi Rih
[ava] Story three, the world. A Russian cargo ship is on fire and drifting in Finnish waters this morning after a drone attack in the Gulf of Finland. ## Technology | sting=tech
// Story 5: Congress nears ban on Chinese cars, Mercedes carve-out
[andrew] Story five, tech. Lawmakers are close to a bipartisan deal to ban Chinese cars from the US for good, according to Bloomberg. Chinese brands like BYD and Geely are already effectively shut out by steep tariffs and Commerce Department rules on Chinese car software. A law would lock that in.
[andrew] The holdup has been Mercedes-Benz. Chinese investors own nearly twenty percent of it, above the fifteen percent limit in the Senate bill. Senators Bernie Moreno of Ohio and Katie Britt of Alabama, where Mercedes builds SUVs, are working on an exception for passive shareholders with no voting power. Supporters say connected cars collect so much data that Chinese control is a security risk. Critics argue it also shields Detroit from cheaper competition, at a time when Europe and Canada are letting Chinese EVs in. Backers want it passed before the new Congress starts in January.
> source: Bloomberg via Transport Topics, "Lawmakers near deal to ban Chinese cars, spare Mercedes" (Nov 20); Yahoo Finance (Nov 20)

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 6: OpenAI bans training rivals; Riemann proof independent check and human rival
[andrew] Story six, AI. OpenAI's new usage rules, which took effect Friday, ban using its models to help train competing AI. That covers more than copying answers. Even grading, ranking or sorting training data for a rival model is off limits. OpenAI says the concern is safety. A copied model could keep the skills but drop the guardrails, for example in cyberattacks. It says new defenses cut the success rate of attacks that try to pull out its models' hidden reasoning from fifty-four percent to under one percent. Critics point out that AI companies trained on vast amounts of other people's work.
[andrew] And the update on the claimed Riemann proof. Independent mathematicians, including Andrew Sutherland at MIT, have now compiled the Lean code themselves without errors. Sutherland told Scientific American the chance anything is fishy is approximately zero. One more check is still pending, to make sure the code proves exactly the right statement. And a twist. Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society, says a young Stanford mathematician, Alessandro Masullo, has been working on his own proof by hand for months. His adviser says that paper is still under major revision, and other mathematicians are skeptical.
> source: OpenAI, "Combatting adversarial distillation" (Nov 20); OpenAI Usage Policies (effective Nov 20); Scientific American, "The AI Riemann hypothesis proof is almost certainly true, independent mathematicians attest" (Nov 20); Scientific American, "Dueling claims of Riemann hypothesis proofs emerge" (Nov 20); Al Jazeera (Nov 21)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 7: FBI NCIC breach; GPG encryption library flaw
[ava] Story seven, the cyber desk. The FBI is investigating an apparent breach of its National Crime Information Center. That's the database police across the country use to check warrants, criminal histories and background checks. CNN first reported it Friday, and NBC and Reuters confirmed it with people briefed on the case. The FBI hasn't commented, and it isn't known what was accessed or who did it. It's the second FBI system breached in recent months, after a September hack of its jobs portal, and it comes the same week as the farm payment breach at the Agriculture Department.
[ava] And a patch worth knowing about. The GnuPG project, which makes widely used encryption tools, warns of a critical flaw in its GPG Made Easy library. A booby-trapped encryption key could crash a program or expose its data. It affects some email programs and Linux software. Update to version two point three point one, or Gpg4win five point one point two on Windows.
> source: NBC News, "FBI investigating potential breach of its crime database" (Nov 20); Reuters via BusinessLine (Nov 21); BBC News (Nov 21); The Hill (Nov 20); GnuPG security advisory, CVE-2026-129975 (Nov 2026); The Hacker News, "GnuPG Warns Critical GPGME Flaw" (Nov 21)

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
// Story 10: CRISPR one-time cholesterol therapy
[ava] And finally, story ten. A single gene-editing infusion cut bad cholesterol roughly in half, and the effect lasted a full year. Doctors at the Cleveland Clinic tested the CRISPR treatment on fifteen patients whose cholesterol didn't respond well to medication. It works by switching off a gene in the liver that raises LDL cholesterol and triglycerides.
[ava] At the highest dose, LDL dropped about fifty-two percent and triglycerides about forty-eight percent, a year after one treatment. There were no serious side effects linked to the therapy. Dr. Luke Laffin called the durability impressive. It's an early, small trial, and bigger studies come next. But picture one visit replacing a lifetime of daily pills.
> source: Good News Network, "First Human Trial to Slash Bad Cholesterol by 50% With One Injection of CRISPR Gene-Editing Therapy" (Nov 21); Cleveland Clinic; New England Journal of Medicine (Aug 2026)
