## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Trump on Fox: credit card cap, FBI hack
[ava] Story one. In a Fox News interview Saturday morning, President Trump said he would gladly work with Democrats to cap credit card interest rates and fees. Senator Elizabeth Warren, expected to chair the Senate Banking Committee when Democrats take over in January, recently said she's ready to work with him on a ten percent cap. The president said card companies make too much and their fees and interest are too high. He acknowledged some would call that a break from free enterprise.
[ava] Supporters say a cap could save consumers billions a year. The card industry and credit unions argue it would push lenders to turn away riskier borrowers, cutting off credit to millions of people. Trump floated a one-year ten percent cap back in January and nothing came of it. It's also unclear how high Democrats will rank the idea.
[ava] In the same interview the president confirmed the hack of the FBI's national crime database. He suggested it might have an upside, saying maybe the country would learn about voter fraud or what he called the Russia hoax. Nothing reported so far ties the breach to those subjects. The FBI still hasn't said what was taken or who was behind it.

// Story 2: Virginia measles death
[ava] Story two. Virginia has reported its first measles-related death this year, a child age four or younger. The state health department did not release the child's vaccination status, citing the family's privacy. Virginia has had two hundred twenty cases this year, mostly among unvaccinated people, with the biggest outbreak in rural Buckingham County. One immunologist noted Virginia hadn't had a measles death since nineteen seventy-eight.
[ava] Nationally, Pennsylvania is the hardest-hit state with more than sixteen hundred cases and eight deaths, and Wisconsin and New York have also reported deaths. The federal CDC counts six deaths so far this year. That's lower than the state tallies because under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Junior the CDC switched to national death records instead of state reports. Health officials stress that two doses of the MMR vaccine are about ninety-seven percent effective.
> source: Virginia Department of Health, "VDH Announces First Measles-Associated Death in 2026" (Nov 20); The Guardian/Reuters, "Virginia child dies in state's first measles-associated death of 2026" (Nov 21); ABC News (Nov 21); NBC4 Washington (Nov 21)

## International | sting=world
// Story 3: Hormuz tanker attacks; F-16 denial
[ava] Story three, the world. More tankers were hit near the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday. Britain's maritime trade agency reported two tankers struck by unidentified projectiles near Khasab, Oman, one in the stern and one in the engine room. No one was hurt. Iran's navy separately said an oil tanker caught fire after hitting a mine in a restricted zone, and accused it of ignoring warnings with its tracking gear switched off. US Central Command flatly denied it, saying Iran did not attack an F-16 at all and every US jet is accounted for. Iran's foreign minister says messages are still passing through mediators. President Trump says Iran wants a deal but not one he can accept.

## Technology | sting=tech
// Story 5: NYT report on Huang's management; Ek backs Europa Fusion
[andrew] Story five, tech. The New York Times has published a deep look at how Jensen Huang ran Nvidia before he resigned last week. Based on more than a dozen current and former employees and twenty years of court records, it says Huang routinely humiliated staff in front of colleagues with insults and violent language. It says he told teams they were too rich to be motivated by money, so humiliation was the way to get more work out of them. Court records show a confidential settlement with an executive who said Huang terrorized staff.
[andrew] The other side is that many employees revere him. Thousands have signed a letter asking for his return, and supporters credit his intensity with building the most valuable chip company in the world.
[andrew] And Spotify founder Daniel Ek is backing a new fusion power startup in Britain called Europa Fusion. Its founder is AI researcher Nantas Nardelli, who previously worked at Google DeepMind. He's betting on heavy computer simulation rather than building lots of test hardware. No one has yet built a fusion plant that puts power on the grid.

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 6: Lean community certifies Riemann proof; UN killer robot talks
[andrew] Story six, AI, and it's official. The Lean and Mathlib communities, the people who maintain the leading computer proof-checking system for mathematics, released a joint statement this morning. They say they have full confidence that Levent Alpöge's AI-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct. That's the hundred sixty-seven year old problem about the pattern of prime numbers.
[andrew] Their checks covered four points. The statement of the problem is right. The seventeen million lines of code compile. The code proves exactly that statement. And several independent proof checkers, some with their own formal guarantees, accepted it. The statement says the next step is for mathematicians to explain the new ideas inside it. The separate human proof from Stanford is still under review.
[andrew] Meanwhile in Geneva, UN-backed talks on AI-powered weapons that can pick and attack targets on their own ended Saturday with a weaker deal than many wanted. Countries agreed to work out a possible agreement by twenty twenty-eight, but did not commit to binding rules. Human rights groups and many countries were disappointed. Reuters reports Russia opposed stronger language, and AP notes the major military powers are jockeying for an edge.
> source: Lean community blog, "Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis" (Nov 21); MIT, RH Lean proof verification files (Nov 21); AP, "Nations agree to extend talks on AI killer robots but no firm commitment to rein them in" (Nov 21); Reuters (Nov 21); Politico Europe (Nov 21)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 7: BADBOX 3.0; N-able outage
[ava] Story seven, the cyber desk. The BADBOX botnet is back and bigger than ever. Researchers at the security firm HUMAN say its third version infects cheap Android TV boxes, tablets and phones, often with malware installed at the factory. At its peak it generated about forty billion fake ad requests a day, playing hidden video ads to defraud advertisers. It also quietly rents out owners' internet connections to proxy networks. HUMAN tied it to a Chinese electronics maker and helped Google take down some of its servers, but warns it will likely adapt. The advice: buy certified Android devices from known brands and make sure Google Play Protect is on.
[ava] And IT management company N-able says hackers broke into one server behind its Mail Assure and SpamExperts email filtering services, causing a worldwide outage since Monday. Mail is still being delivered and filtered, but customer portals are down. N-able says it has found no sign that customer mailboxes were accessed, and it hasn't set a date for full restoration.
> source: BleepingComputer, "BADBOX botnet returns with record-breaking 40 billion daily ad requests" (Nov 21); HUMAN Satori Threat Intelligence (Nov 2026); BleepingComputer, "N-able says recent security breach led to major service outage" (Nov 20); SecurityWeek (Nov 20); CRN (Nov 2026)

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
// Story 10: Whale genome from drone-caught breath
[ava] And finally, story ten. Scientists have read a whale's entire genome from its breath. An international team, including researchers from the University of Saint Andrews in Scotland, flew a small drone with sterile dishes through the spouts of humpback whales off northern British Columbia. They gathered fifty-eight breath samples from twenty-six whales, more than a third of them flown straight from shore without a boat.
[ava] No darts, no biopsies, no disturbing the animals. The breath DNA matched what traditional sampling gives, correctly identified every whale's sex, and turned up three new genetic variants. Lead researcher Éadin O'Mahony says they'll use it to learn whether whales that feed together are family or just longtime friends. One exhale can even reveal the microbes in a whale's airways, a window into its health.
> source: Good News Network, "Breath of Whale Captured by Drone Allows Full Genome Sequencing Without Intrusion" (Nov 21); Molecular Ecology Resources; University of St Andrews
