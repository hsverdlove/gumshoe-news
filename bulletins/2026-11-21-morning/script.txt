## US News | sting=us
// Story 1: Common Cents Act signed; penny retired
[ava] Story one. The penny is officially done. President Trump on Friday signed the Common Cents Act, which permanently ends making pennies for general circulation. The Mint actually stopped producing them a year ago, and says each one cost nearly four cents to make.
[ava] Your pennies are still legal tender, and the Federal Reserve will keep circulating the roughly one hundred fourteen billion already out there. The law also sets rules for cash purchases. Totals get rounded to the nearest nickel, so ten dollars and two cents becomes ten dollars, and ten dollars and four cents becomes ten oh five. Retailers welcomed it, saying stores had faced lawsuits over not giving exact change. The bill passed both chambers by voice vote.
> source: The White House, "H.R. 10167 – Common Cents Act" (Nov 20); NBC News, "Trump formally ends penny minting after nearly 250 years of production" (Nov 20); CBS News, "Trump signs Common Cents Act into law" (Nov 20); Congress.gov, H.R. 10167

// Story 2: Johnson calls absent Republicans back; lame-duck crunch
[ava] Story two. House Speaker Mike Johnson has a blunt message for his own members: show up. In a letter Friday, he told Republicans that barring a serious medical issue, every one of them should be in Washington for the forty-four days left before Democrats take over. About twenty Republicans missed votes Thursday, forcing leaders to postpone a key vote and cancel Friday's session. Many of them lost their races.

Others in the party blame Senate Majority Leader John Thune for the slow pace, which Thune disputes. Meanwhile, incoming House Majority Leader Katherine Clark released next year's calendar, with about a hundred twenty-seven voting days, which Democrats say shows they'll hit the ground running.

// Story 4: COP31 still deadlocked on Saturday
[ava] Story four. The UN climate summit in Antalya Turkey is still going this morning, a day past its deadline, with work crews already boxing up the venue. The sticking point remains the draft deal, which dropped every mention of fossil fuels after pushback led by Saudi Arabia. Panama's environment minister says a deal that doesn't even mention fossil fuels is unacceptable, and France has rejected language that leans on carbon capture.
[ava] One possible trade on the table: keep fossil fuels out of the text in exchange for oil-producing countries signing up to get thirty-five percent of their energy from electricity by twenty thirty-five. Critics note that target doesn't require the power to come from renewables. African countries are also fighting to restore a hundred twenty billion dollar target for adaptation money, which was cut. Australia's Chris Bowen, who chairs the talks, says he'll stay as long as it takes, and a new draft is expected today.

## Artificial Intelligence | sting=ai
// Story 6: Trump threatens tax on AI data centers abroad; Riemann proof first reactions
[andrew] Story six, AI. At his news conference Friday, President Trump said American companies that build AI data centers in other countries will face what he called a tremendous tax. He said he hadn't even told his staff yet. He gave no rate, no legal mechanism and no timeline. Supporters say it keeps AI investment and jobs at home. Critics note that Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle already run data centers abroad, often to serve local customers or meet local laws.
[andrew] Now, the follow-up on that claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The mathematician Levent Alpöge says Anthropic's models produced it as seventeen point four million lines of code in Lean, a system that checks proofs by computer. Terence Tao, perhaps the world's best-known mathematician, called it enormous. He says it appears to draw on some of the deepest modern number theory, and that digesting it will take time. Computer scientist Scott Aaronson bets it will stand, since no AI result checked by Lean has had to be retracted. But he warns that Lean itself has had bugs, and that humans may need years to truly understand the argument. So, promising, but not yet confirmed.
> source: CNBC, "Trump vows to stop U.S. companies from building data centers abroad, threatens 'tremendous tax'" (Nov 20); AP (Nov 20); Axios (Nov 20); Scott Aaronson, Shtetl-Optimized, "The stochastic parrots have apparently proved the Riemann Hypothesis" (Nov 20); BBC News (Nov 20); reports on Terence Tao's comments (Nov 20)

## Cybersecurity | sting=cyber
// Story 7: USDA farm payment breach; Bell Canada "agentic" attacker
[ava] Story seven, the cyber desk. The Agriculture Department has confirmed a cyber incident that knocked out farm loan, payment and crop insurance systems for most of the week. NBC News reports hackers got into the system that sends payments to farmers. That would make it the first outside break-in among this month's string of federal tech outages. USDA Undersecretary Richard Fordyce says no personal information was involved and no data was lost, because the protections worked. Former officials told NBC they worry the system could be a path to more sensitive data. Most systems were due back by Friday evening, with weekend work if needed.
[ava] And Canada's Bell says what it calls an agentic attacker broke into its systems on Tuesday. That term suggests AI tools did much of the work. The intruders took business contact details for enterprise customers from a system used to order network circuits. Bell says no passwords or sensitive personal data were taken, and service wasn't affected.

## Uplifting Story | sting=feel
// Story 10: Gloucester schoolchildren's letters to a wounded Vietnam corpsman
[ava] And finally, story ten. In nineteen sixty-seven, Navy corpsman Russell Jacobson came home from Vietnam with twelve gunshot wounds and grenade shrapnel. At Chelsea Naval Hospital outside Boston, he was in a full-body cast. He had stopped talking, and he was refusing food. His weight fell to about eighty pounds.
[ava] A fellow nurse's sister was a nun at Saint Ann School in Gloucester, and she had her students write to him. Thirty-three letters arrived. One boy wrote about his pet lizard. One asked how the Montreal Expos were doing. Those letters are what started to bring him back.
[ava] Nearly sixty years later, his son Dave is tracking down every letter writer to tell them it mattered. He's found eighteen so far, including the nun who organized it, now ninety. One woman told him she does remember, and still feels a little guilty she didn't write more. Dave is still looking for the other fifteen.
> source: Good News Network, "33 Kids Wrote Letters to a Wounded Veteran. 60 Years Later, His Son Is Searching to Thank Each One" (Nov 20); The Letters from Gloucester project site
